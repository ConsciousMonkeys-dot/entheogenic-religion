# The Seven Deadly Sins and the Medicine

## The thesis

The seven deadly sins are not a list of crimes. They are a map of the human condition — the patterns that keep a person from God, from others, and from themselves. The medicine does not condemn these patterns. It reveals them. And what is revealed can finally be healed.

The tradition has always known the sins are connected. Pride is the root; the others grow from it. But the modern understanding goes further: these patterns are not chosen freely. They are the fruit of wounds. A person who is chronically angry was once powerless. A person who is envious was once made to feel small. A person who is greedy was once made to feel unsafe. The sin is the scar tissue over the trauma — and the medicine is what lets you see the wound beneath it.

## The mirror

The medicine's deepest gift is the mirror. Under its influence, the defenses fall. The stories you tell yourself about who you are dissolve, and what remains is what is actually there — including how others see you. Not the flattering version. Not the version you perform. The version that has been watching you all along, quietly, from the eyes of everyone you have ever hurt, ignored, or failed.

This is not punishment. This is the first honest look. And it is the beginning of repentance — not the repentance of guilt, but the repentance of recognition. You cannot turn from a pattern you cannot see. The medicine shows you the pattern. Then the work begins.

## Sin by sin

### Pride (superbia)

Pride is the belief that you are above correction, above others, above God. It is the sin that makes all the others invisible, because the proud person cannot see themselves clearly enough to notice them.

The medicine dissolves the self-image. The ego that says "I am the center" temporarily steps aside, and the person sees themselves as one being among many — small, mortal, connected. This is the mystical experience Griffiths documented: the dissolution of self, the unity with all things. Pride cannot survive that view. What remains is humility — not self-abasement, but accurate self-knowledge.

### Greed (avaritia)

Greed is the fear of not having enough, dressed up as ambition. It is the wound of scarcity — the child who learned that love, safety, or food could be taken away, and who decided to never be without again.

The medicine reveals the fear underneath the grasping. When the defenses drop, the person sees that the accumulation was never about the things. It was about the terror of emptiness. And in the medicine's space — where time slows and the self dissolves — the person often experiences a fullness that no possession could provide. The greed loosens its grip because the fear that fed it has been seen.

### Lust (luxuria)

Lust is desire detached from personhood. It is the wound of not being truly seen — the person who learned that their body was the only thing that got attention, or that intimacy was dangerous, and who now seeks the sensation without the vulnerability.

The medicine brings the person face to face with what they actually want: not the body, but to be known. To be held without being used. The encounter with the other — the entities Luke's participants met, the presence Griffiths' volunteers described — is often an encounter with unconditional regard. For the first time, the person is seen completely, and nothing is taken from them. That experience rewires the longing. Desire returns to its proper object: connection, not consumption.

### Envy (invidia)

Envy is the pain of another's good fortune. It is the wound of comparison — the child who was measured against a sibling, a neighbor, an ideal, and found wanting. Envy says: if they have it, I cannot.

The medicine collapses the comparison. In the unity experience, the boundary between self and other thins or disappears. The other's good is no longer a threat; it is part of the same field. Envy requires separation to survive. The medicine removes the separation, and with it, the envy.

What remains is often grief — for the years spent measuring yourself against others instead of living your own life. That grief is the healing beginning.

### Gluttony (gula)

Gluttony is not about food. It is about filling a void that cannot be filled by consumption. It is the wound of emptiness — the person who was never nourished emotionally, and who learned to seek comfort in what goes in the mouth.

The medicine reveals the emptiness directly. Not as an abstraction, but as a felt absence. And in that space, the person can finally ask: what was I actually hungry for? The answer is rarely food. It is presence. Attention. Being enough. When that is seen, the compulsive filling loses its power — not through willpower, but through understanding.

### Wrath (ira)

Wrath is the most visible sin, and the most misunderstood. It is not anger itself — anger is information, a signal that a boundary was crossed. Wrath is anger that has been stored, compressed, and redirected. It is the wound of powerlessness — the person who could not fight back when they were hurt, and who now carries that fight in their body.

The medicine unlocks what was stored. The crying you described — the deep, body-level release — is often the completion of a survival response that was interrupted decades ago. The person finally gets to feel the anger they were not allowed to feel, in a container where it cannot harm anyone. And when it passes through, what remains is not rage but grief, and then peace.

This is why wrath cannot be healed by suppression or by punishment. It must be felt, fully, in safety. The medicine provides the access. The container provides the safety.

### Sloth (acedia)

Sloth is not laziness. In the original tradition it is *acedia* — spiritual despair, the heaviness of the soul that cannot see meaning. It is the wound of hopelessness — the person who has been through so much that nothing seems worth the effort.

The medicine is the direct antidote to acedia, because it produces the opposite experience: overwhelming meaning. The mystical experience is not abstract — it is felt as the most real thing the person has ever encountered. Purpose, connection, the sense that life matters. Griffiths' volunteers rated these experiences among the most significant of their lives, and the changes persisted for years.

Sloth says nothing matters. The medicine shows you that everything matters. The despair cannot survive that demonstration.

## The pattern underneath

Look at all seven and the pattern is the same: each sin is a strategy for surviving a wound. Pride protects from shame. Greed protects from scarcity. Lust protects from rejection. Envy protects from feeling small. Gluttony protects from emptiness. Wrath protects from powerlessness. Sloth protects from meaninglessness.

The medicine does not remove the protection by force. It reveals the wound, so the protection is no longer needed. This is the difference between moralism and healing. Moralism says: stop doing that. Healing says: I see why you did that. And when the why is seen, the doing stops on its own.

## The theological point

The tradition has always treated the sins as things to be confessed and forgiven. That is true, but it is incomplete. Confession without understanding is just another performance — another way to manage the image rather than heal the person.

The medicine completes the confession. It does not just name the sin. It shows you where it came from, what it was protecting, and what it cost you. That is the repentance the tradition has always pointed toward but rarely reached: not the turning away from sin, but the turning toward the wound that produced it.

And this is why the medicine belongs in the sacred, not the criminal. It is doing the work the tradition has always asked of its people — seeing yourself clearly, turning from what harms, returning to what is real. The only difference is that the medicine does it in hours what the tradition took lifetimes to do.

## The caveat

The mirror cuts both ways. A person who is not ready — who has no container, no guide, no intention — can be shattered by what they see. The sins are not just patterns to be revealed; they are also defenses, and defenses exist for a reason. This is why the container matters as much as the medicine. The revelation without the holding is not healing. It is harm.

The medicine is the key. The container is the door. The intention is the hand that turns it.
