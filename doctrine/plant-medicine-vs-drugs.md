# Plant Medicine vs. Drugs

## The Distinction

The dividing line is not plant versus synthetic. It is **addiction potential** and the come-down.

- **Drugs** — addictive, create tolerance and withdrawal, produce a high followed by an equal low. Cocaine, heroin, alcohol. The user wants more; the body demands more.
- **Plant medicine** — non-addictive, non-toxic, self-limiting. Psilocybin, DMT, cannabis. No come-down. You come up, you come off, you are normal.

## The Evidence

- **Heroin** is a plant derivative — opium poppy — and the most addictive substance known.
- **Alcohol** is fermented fruit, and the most destructive drug in society by harm statistics.
- **MDMA** is synthetic and non-addictive.
- **Ketamine** is synthetic and used as a medical treatment.
- **Psilocybin** builds no tolerance — the dose that works today works the same in a year.
- **DMT** clears the body in roughly twenty minutes. A person can be on another planet and drive home half an hour later.

## The Inconsistency

The state bans the one class of substances that does not create dependence, while alcohol and tobacco — the two most addictive and most harmful — remain legal. That is the inconsistency this religion points at.

## Different Drinks, Different Consciousness

The state does not ban consciousness-altering substances — it bans the ones that produce *unmanageable* consciousness. Different drinks steer different states, and the research confirms it:

- **Spirits (gin, vodka, whiskey)** — linked to aggression, tearfulness, and depression. Nearly a third of spirit drinkers in a global survey associated them with aggression, compared with around 2.5% of red wine drinkers. Nearly a quarter said spirits left them tearful, versus 17% for red wine and 9% for beer and white wine. Only 20% said spirits made them feel relaxed.
- **Red wine** — linked to relaxation. Over half of red wine drinkers said they felt relaxed after it, and it was the least linked to aggression of any drink.
- **Beer** — also linked to relaxation, roughly 50%.

Source: the largest study of its kind — almost 30,000 people aged 18 to 34 across 21 countries, published in BMJ Open (2017), led by Professor Mark Bellis of Public Health Wales. The study measured self-reported emotions, not causation, but the pattern is consistent with centuries of cultural observation: spirits have a history laced with violence; wine has a history of conviviality.

The implication for this doctrine: the state tolerates the drink that makes people angry and nasty, and bans the one that produces clarity and direct experience. The ban is not about public health — it is about which consciousness is permitted.

## Medical Evidence: Plant Medicine Treating Addiction

The medical world has proven that these substances can help people break serious addictions — including heroin. This is the strongest practical argument for the religion: the sacrament heals what the legal drugs destroy.

### Ibogaine (from the iboga plant, Tabernanthe iboga)

- **October 2026**: The US FDA advanced the first government-funded clinical trials of ibogaine for opioid addiction and PTSD, with the Department of Health and Human Services funding early-stage studies through ARPA-H and NIDA. This followed an April 2026 executive order dedicating $50 million to federal ibogaine research.
- **Texas IMPACT trial**: $50 million appropriated in June 2025 — the largest public commitment to psychedelic research in US history — with UTHealth Houston and UTMB Health co-leading trials for opioid use disorder.
- **Stanford MISTIC trial** (Nature Medicine, 2024): 30 special-operations veterans received a magnesium-ibogaine protocol. PTSD symptoms fell 88%, depression fell 87%, with no serious cardiac adverse events.
- **Real-world data**: Beond Health in Mexico has delivered 6,000+ medically supervised ibogaine sessions, with 90% of patients self-reporting success and a 71% abstinence rate at 6 months in Stanford's expanded Phase II opioid study.
- **MAPS-funded study**: 50% opioid abstinence rate at 30 days in a medically supervised setting.
- **Observational data** (Davis et al. 2017): a meaningful subset of people who used ibogaine for opioid problems reported sustained reductions at 12 months.
- **Caveat**: a 2026 review in the Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology found no double-blind RCT has yet demonstrated efficacy for opioid use disorder — most positive data comes from uncontrolled studies with high risk of bias. Cardiac risk (QT prolongation) remains the key safety hurdle.

### Psilocybin (from mushrooms)

- **National Survey on Drug Use and Health** (214,505 US adults, 2015–2019, published in Scientific Reports 2022): lifetime psilocybin use was associated with 30% lower odds of opioid use disorder (adjusted odds ratio 0.70). No other classic psychedelic showed this link.
- **Johns Hopkins**: Phase 2 randomized controlled trial of psilocybin for opioid use disorder in patients on methadone maintenance.
- **Imperial College London** (David Nutt): the PsilOpioid trial — a proof-of-concept RCT testing whether psilocybin therapy reduces return to opioid use after detox.
- **University of Pennsylvania** (Wellcome Leap): Phase 2 trial of psilocybin for OUD patients on medication-assisted treatment.
- **Psilocybin for tobacco use disorder**: earlier trials (Johnson et al. 2014) showed strong smoking-cessation results — one of the most robust findings in the field.

### Ayahuasca (contains DMT from the acacia/chacruna)

- **Takiwasi Centre, Peru**: one-year follow-up of an ayahuasca-assisted integrative addiction program showed significant reductions in alcohol and drug use severity, depression, anxiety, and craving.
- **British Columbia, Canada** (Thomas et al. 2013, MAPS-funded): a five-day retreat combining ayahuasca ceremonies with group counselling produced statistically significant improvements in hopefulness, empowerment, mindfulness, and quality of life; self-reported alcohol, tobacco, and cocaine use declined.
- **Systematic reviews** (2016–2020 update, European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience): replicated findings of therapeutic effects on substance use disorders, though noting the need for randomized controlled trials.

### The Pattern

A single supervised session of ibogaine can interrupt opioid withdrawal and reduce craving for months — unlike daily-dose maintenance drugs like methadone. Psilocybin is associated with lower rates of opioid addiction across a quarter-million-person survey. Ayahuasca-assisted therapy reduces substance use in ritual and clinical settings.

The implication for this doctrine: the medical establishment is now racing to study the very substances the law bans — because they work where conventional treatment fails. The religion's sacrament is the medicine the hospitals are trying to catch up with.
