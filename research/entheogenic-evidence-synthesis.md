# The Entheogenic Evidence: A Synthesis of Modern Psychedelic Research

**A research paper for the entheogenic-religion project**

*Prepared for ConsciousMonkeys-dot / entheogenic-religion*
*October 2026*

---

## Abstract

This paper synthesizes the findings of six major research programs — David Luke (University of Greenwich), Rick Strassman (University of New Mexico), Roland Griffiths (Johns Hopkins), Charles Grob (UCLA), Robin Carhart-Harris (Imperial College London), Chris Timmermann (Imperial College London), and Dennis McKenna (Hoasca Project, Brazil) — and argues that their results converge on a coherent picture: psychedelic substances reliably produce experiences that participants describe as encounters with something other than themselves, and these experiences produce lasting, measurable changes in personality, values, and behavior. The paper does not claim to prove that the entities encountered are external or divine. It argues that the convergence of independent findings across continents, substances, and methodologies is strong enough to demand a serious theological and philosophical response — and that the traditional dismissal of these experiences as mere hallucination is no longer scientifically tenable.

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question Behind the Question

Every religion in history has claimed that its founders encountered something real — a voice, a presence, a vision. Moses at the burning bush. Muhammad in the cave of Hira. Paul on the road to Damascus. The question that has always divided believers from skeptics is whether these encounters were with something external or with the mind itself.

For most of the twentieth century, the scientific answer was simple: hallucination. The experiences were real as experiences, but they pointed at nothing. The brain, under chemical stress, generated false perceptions.

That answer is now under pressure. Not from theology, but from clinical research. Over the past thirty years, independent laboratories on three continents have administered controlled doses of psychedelic substances to thousands of volunteers and measured what happens. The results are consistent, replicable, and — for anyone building a religion around plant medicine — deeply relevant.

This paper gathers those results and asks what they mean together.

## 2. The Researchers and Their Programs

### 2.1 David Luke — University of Greenwich, London

Luke runs the Psychedelic and Exceptional Experience Lab (PEEL). His approach is distinctive: he studies experienced users in naturalistic settings rather than naive volunteers in clinics, and he takes the content of the experiences seriously rather than reducing them to neurochemistry.

**Key findings:**

- **DMT entity encounters.** In his field studies with Pascal Michael, experienced DMT users reported contact with beings — described as aliens, mantises, elves, guides, ancestors — at rates far higher than chance would predict if the experiences were random. The 2021 paper "An Encounter With the Other" (Frontiers in Psychology) and the 2023 "An Encounter With the Self" documented these reports in detail. A 2025 PLOS ONE paper compared DMT experiences with near-death experiences and found structural similarities: both involve encounters with seemingly autonomous entities, life reviews, and a sense of entering another reality.

- **The brain as filter.** Luke's theoretical framework, developed across his books (Otherworlds, DMT Dialogues, DMT Entity Encounters), draws on Aldous Huxley's perennial philosophy: the brain does not produce consciousness but filters it. Psychedelics reduce the filter. On this model, the entities are not generated by the drug — they are revealed by it. This is the single most important theoretical link between Luke's work and the religious traditions that treat plant medicine as a sacrament: the medicine is not a hallucinogen but a **revealer**.

- **Psychedelic psi.** Luke's experiments on precognition under DMT produced above-chance scoring, though with small samples (around twenty per condition). He has also documented "psychedelic shamanism" — the use of psychedelics in magical and divinatory practice — and "ecodelia," the phenomenon where psychedelic experiences increase ecological concern and connection to nature.

- **The LSD creative problem-solving trial.** Luke's most publicized work: approximately fifty PhD scientists from Imperial, Cambridge, and Oxford, dosed at around seventy-five micrograms in a North London hospital, each bringing an unsolved problem. Luke reports that divergent thinking rose sharply while convergent thinking collapsed, and that multiple participants experienced breakthroughs — a materials scientist who visualized a solution to programmable chain-mail fabric (later patented and developed with NASA and MIT), a tectonic-plates researcher who resolved three years of incoherent data, and Merlin Sheldrake, whose experience of "turning into the fungus" fed into Entangled Life. The study has not been fully published; only pharmacokinetics have appeared. No control group exists. But the pattern — insight arriving during the experience, not after — matches the 1966 Harman and Fadiman study of twenty-seven professionals, which found similar illumination-phase effects.

- **Embedded precognition sub-study.** Within the LSD trial, Luke secretly embedded a precognition task. Thirteen participants scored at chance off LSD but significantly below chance on it — which Luke interprets as "sign-missing," the tendency of skeptical scientists to unconsciously avoid correct answers. This is a provocative but unreplicated finding.

### 2.2 Rick Strassman — University of New Mexico

Strassman ran the first modern American DMT study, 1990–1995: approximately four hundred intravenous doses to sixty volunteers. His 2001 book *DMT: The Spirit Molecule* remains the foundational text.

**Key findings:**

- **Entity contact was the norm, not the exception.** Volunteers reported beings — aliens, mantises, elves, insectoid entities, guides — with a frequency that surprised even Strassman. He had expected hallucinations; he got encounters.

- **No tolerance.** Unlike most psychoactive substances, DMT showed no tolerance across repeated sessions. This is pharmacologically unusual and theologically suggestive: if the substance were merely producing a chemical state, repeated exposure should blunt it.

- **The pineal hypothesis.** Strassman proposed that the pineal gland produces endogenous DMT and that this may be the biological basis of mystical and near-death experiences. Trace DMT has been found in rat pineals; it has never been confirmed in humans at psychedelic levels. The hypothesis remains unproven but has driven a decade of follow-up research.

- **Theological framing.** Strassman, trained as a psychiatrist, explicitly framed his work in terms of the soul and the afterlife. He asked whether DMT experiences could be windows into a reality that survives death. His later work (*DMT and the Soul of Prophecy*) argued that the Hebrew prophets' experiences resemble DMT experiences in structure.

### 2.3 Roland Griffiths — Johns Hopkins University

Griffiths ran the landmark psilocybin studies that moved psychedelic research from the fringe to the mainstream.

**Key findings:**

- **The 2006 study.** Thirty volunteers, moderate doses of psilocybin. At two months, participants rated the experience among the five most spiritually significant events of their lives; at fourteen months, that rating held. Sixty-seven percent rated it among the five most personally meaningful; sixty-four percent among the five most spiritually significant.

- **Dose-response (2011).** Seventy-two percent of participants at high doses had a "complete mystical experience" as measured by standard scales (the Mystical Experience Questionnaire). The proportion rose with dose.

- **Personality change (2017).** A follow-up found lasting increases in openness, gratitude, forgiveness, death transcendence, and prosocial behavior — changes larger than those normally seen over decades of aging. These were not transient mood effects; they persisted.

- **The mystical experience as the active ingredient.** Griffiths' most important theoretical contribution: the degree of mystical experience predicted the degree of lasting benefit. Not the drug dose alone — the quality of the encounter. This is the finding that most directly supports the sacramental model: the medicine works through the experience it produces, not through a chemical side effect.

### 2.4 Charles Grob — UCLA

Grob gave psilocybin to twelve terminal cancer patients (2008–2011), measuring anxiety and depression.

**Key findings:**

- Anxiety dropped significantly at one and three months; depression improved at six months.
- No bad trips, no adverse events, no psychological harm.
- Patients reported reduced fear of death and increased acceptance.

This study is the strongest evidence that psychedelic experiences can address the deepest human fear — mortality — in a clinical population. It is also the study most directly relevant to any religion that promises something about death.

### 2.5 Robin Carhart-Harris — Imperial College London

Carhart-Harris ran the largest modern depression trial: psilocybin versus escitalopram (2021).

**Key findings:**

- Remission rates were twice as high with psilocybin: fifty-seven percent versus twenty-eight percent.
- The psilocybin group showed greater increases in emotional breakthrough and psychological insight.

This is the finding that moved psychedelics from "interesting" to "medically serious" in the eyes of mainstream psychiatry. It also demonstrates that the same substances produce both mystical experiences and clinical benefit — the two are not separate phenomena.

### 2.6 Chris Timmermann — Imperial College London

Timmermann's 2023 study is the most detailed brain imaging of DMT to date: twenty milligrams IV, simultaneous fMRI and EEG in twenty volunteers.

**Key findings:**

- **Superior temporal lobe activation correlated specifically with the experience of entities.** The brain region associated with social cognition and detecting other minds lit up when participants reported encountering beings.
- **Hippocampal and medial parietal deactivation tracked the dissolution of self, time, and space.** The regions that maintain the sense of "I am here, now" went quiet.

This is the single most important neuroscientific finding for the entheogenic religion project: the brain changes that accompany entity encounters are specific and measurable. The experience of "the other" is not a generic hallucination — it has a distinct neural signature.

### 2.7 Dennis McKenna — Hoasca Project, Brazil

McKenna studied long-term members of the UDV (União do Vegetal), a Brazilian church that uses ayahuasca as its sacrament.

**Key findings:**

- No toxicity, no organ damage, no cognitive decline in long-term users.
- Better memory scores than matched controls.
- Lasting behavioral change, including resolution of alcoholism and other addictions.
- Improved family relationships and community cohesion.

This is the only study of a functioning religious community using plant medicine as sacrament over decades. It answers the question every skeptic asks: what happens when people use these substances regularly, in community, for spiritual purposes? The answer: they get better, not worse.

## 3. The Convergence: What the Findings Mean Together

No single study proves anything about the nature of reality. But six independent programs, on three continents, using different substances (DMT, psilocybin, LSD, ayahuasca), different populations (naive volunteers, experienced users, terminal patients, depressed patients, religious practitioners), and different methods (clinical trials, field studies, brain imaging, community observation) converge on the same picture:

**1. Entity encounters are common and structured.** Across Luke's field studies, Strassman's clinical trials, and Timmermann's imaging, participants report contact with seemingly autonomous beings. The reports are consistent across substances and cultures. Timmermann's finding that superior temporal lobe activation tracks entity experience specifically suggests this is not a generic confabulation.

**2. The experiences produce lasting change.** Griffiths' personality data, Grob's anxiety reduction, Carhart-Harris's depression remission, and McKenna's community findings all show that these are not transient states. They alter people permanently.

**3. The mystical quality predicts the benefit.** Griffiths' finding that the degree of mystical experience predicts lasting improvement is the bridge between the clinical and the spiritual. The medicine works through the encounter, not around it.

**4. No tolerance, no toxicity, no harm in religious use.** Strassman's no-tolerance finding and McKenna's long-term community data contradict the "dangerous drug" model. These substances behave pharmacologically like sacraments, not like intoxicants.

**5. The brain-as-filter model is the most parsimonious framework.** Luke's theoretical contribution ties it together: if the brain filters consciousness rather than producing it, then psychedelics are not creating experiences but removing obstacles. This explains why the experiences feel like encounters rather than inventions, why they are consistent across cultures, and why they produce lasting spiritual change.

## 4. The Counterarguments and the Honest Limits

A serious paper must state what it cannot claim.

**No control for expectation.** None of these studies fully separates the drug from the set and setting. Participants expect mystical experiences; they may produce them.

**No proof of externality.** The neural correlates of entity experience (Timmermann) show what the brain does during the experience. They do not show that the entity is outside the brain. The superior temporal lobe activates when you see a face in a crowd — it does not prove the face is real.

**Small samples in key studies.** Luke's psi findings (n≈20), Grob's cancer study (n=12), and parts of Strassman's work are underpowered by modern standards.

**Publication gaps.** Luke's LSD trial is unpublished beyond pharmacokinetics. Some of the most dramatic claims rest on interviews and self-report.

**The pineal hypothesis is unproven.** Strassman's endogenous DMT idea has not been confirmed in humans.

These limits are real. They mean the evidence supports a *theological hypothesis* — that these substances open access to something real — but does not yet prove it. The honest position is: the evidence is strong enough to take the hypothesis seriously, and too weak to dismiss it.

## 5. Implications for the Entheogenic Religion Project

If the convergence holds, several implications follow for building a religion around plant medicine:

**The sacrament is not symbolic.** In Christianity, bread and wine point to a reality beyond themselves. In the entheogenic model, the medicine *is* the access. Luke's brain-as-filter framework and Griffiths' mystical-experience-predicts-benefit finding both support treating the substance as the direct means of encounter, not a metaphor for it.

**The entity encounter is the core religious event.** Across all the research, the most transformative experiences involve contact with something other. A religion built on this evidence should center the encounter — preparation, intention, community, integration — rather than the substance alone.

**The changes are the evidence of authenticity.** McKenna's UDV findings show that decades of sacramental use produce stable, positive communities. The religion's credibility rests not on doctrine but on the measurable transformation of its practitioners.

**The legal and medical case is building.** Carhart-Harris's depression data and Grob's cancer data are moving regulators. The religious freedom argument (UDV in the US, Santo Daime in multiple countries) now has clinical backing.

---

## 6. Conclusion

The twentieth century dismissed entheogenic experience as chemical noise. The twenty-first century's research has not restored the old certainties — it has replaced them with a harder question: if the brain, under the influence of specific substances, reliably produces encounters that feel like meeting something real, and if those encounters reliably transform the people who have them, what is the most honest response?

The researchers in this paper did not set out to prove God. They set out to measure what happens. What they found was that the experiences people have always called divine are reproducible, structured, and transformative. Whether that means the divine is real, or that the human mind is far stranger than we thought, is the question this religion project exists to answer.
